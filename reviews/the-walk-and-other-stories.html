The Walk and Other Stories

AuthorRobert Walser
TypeShort fiction / novella
First published1957 (English collection)
Original1917 (Der Spaziergang / The Walk)
PublisherJohn Calder (1957 edition)

Within the first few pages of Walser, I had the distinct impression that this was a man who had suffered considerably, so much so that the vulgar confirmation of a Wikipedia article seemed almost unnecessary. There is something faintly distressed about even his happiest passages, a nervous excess of courtesy, an eagerness to diminish himself, a delight in ordinary things so extravagant that one begins to suspect how badly he must have needed them. His narrators seem forever apologizing for their existence while quietly resenting the necessity of doing so. And yet how lovingly he writes of the world, of the trees, the light, the passing faces of strangers, all those little things which scarcely survive the moment of their occurrence. I wonder whether his tenderness toward the world was made possible, in part, by his difficulty inhabiting himself.

The Walk is a story of almost nothing happening, although Walser manages to make this nothing into an inexhaustible subject. A writer leaves his room, wanders through town, visits a few shops, quarrels with a tax official, admires the countryside, and returns home. His thoughts go everywhere his feet do not. An ordinary encounter swells into some elaborate meditation, only to collapse again into an absurdity, and then off he goes, delighted by something else. I particularly loved his defense of walking as a legitimate occupation, an argument which must have been rather personal to a man whose profession consisted largely of wandering about and noticing things. How terribly difficult it is to justify doing nothing in a world so preoccupied with doing something… Though Walser, of course, was never doing nothing. He was busy looking.

His shorter pieces are even less concerned with arriving anywhere. Many occupy scarcely a page or two, little scraps of thought in which people speak, complain, fall in love, humiliate themselves, and disappear almost before we’ve become acquainted with them. The writing has a lovely, unsettled quality, drifting between sincerity and satire, with the occasional impression that Walser himself has forgotten whether he is being serious. I especially admire his humor, which seems to arise from the disproportion between the triviality of an occasion and the enormous seriousness with which his characters receive it. A minor embarrassment becomes a catastrophe of dignity, an insignificant grievance occasions some grand declaration, and his narrators are forever making themselves ridiculous in their efforts to be regarded with a little respect. Walser seems to understand how much of our suffering is sustained by our own vanity, how desperately we wish to preserve some respectable image of ourselves even as our behavior makes a mockery of it. His comedy is rarely cruel, though neither is it entirely consoling; the characters are allowed to expose themselves in all their foolishness without being rescued from it. Perhaps this is why I find his humor so endearing. He seems to regard human absurdity with an affection which never quite becomes indulgence.

And what beautiful prose. There is a passage in The Boat which I found especially fantastic:

No noise, no sound. Everything is wrapped in a black, sweet silence. The stars tremble high above in the sky and also upward from far below out of the sky which lies on the surface of the water. The water is the friend of the moon, it has pulled it down to itself, and now they kiss, the water and the moon, like boyfriend and girlfriend. The beautiful moon has sunk into the water like a daring young prince into a flood of peril.

— “The Boat”

I love the almost childlike sincerity of this, how effortlessly the moon becomes a lover and the water its sweetheart, and how little Walser seems to care whether the image might strike someone as terribly sentimental. There is something enviable about his willingness to be enchanted by the world, to indulge in its little absurdities without feeling obliged to explain them away. How much lovelier life might seem if one allowed oneself such useless pleasures more often; took a train to some faraway town for no particular reason, wandered through the countryside, or stopped to admire a perfectly ordinary thing without demanding that it amount to anything.

My reservation is with the fragments, though not with their brevity or lack of plot, since some of Walser’s loveliest writing occupies scarcely a sentence. Rather, too many of them seem content with the first pleasing turn of a thought. An observation is made, a character indulges in some charming foolishness, a little irony is introduced, and then the piece ends before anything has been sufficiently troubled or complicated by what preceded it. Individually these are often delightful, but read in succession their gestures become dulling; the same playful evasions, the same sudden tenderness, the same whimsical reversals which initially seemed so spontaneous begin to feel almost habitual. I found myself growing impatient with his refusal to remain with an idea long enough for it to acquire any resistance. A fragment needn’t resolve itself, of course, but neither should its incompleteness be mistaken for profundity. At times the writing seems to rely too heavily on the charm of its own inconsequence, as though the slightest impression, rendered beautifully enough, were already sufficient. And perhaps it is, occasionally. But I think Walser is at his best in the longer pieces, where his digressions are allowed to accumulate and contradict one another, where the humor gradually acquires something of sadness beneath it.

I love Walser, though. There is something so intimate about reading him, as though one were being allowed to overhear the thoughts of a person who has forgotten to be embarrassed by them. His narrators are vain, timid, tender, occasionally insufferable, and so wonderfully alive to the smallest things. I know very well that the man who writes is not necessarily the man being written, but I couldn’t help feeling strangely acquainted with him by the end.
