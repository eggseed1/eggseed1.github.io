Shadow Ticket

AuthorThomas Pynchon
TypeNovel
Published7 October 2025
PublisherPenguin Press

I bought Shadow Ticket on its release day, though I kept putting off reading it because of the other books on my list. And when I finally got around to it, I found myself taking an extraordinarily long time to finish what is, by Pynchon’s standards, a rather straightforward novel. The plot is considerably less difficult to follow than those of his more ambitious works, the prose less forbidding, and yet I think this may have been the most laborious reading experience I’ve had with him. I could hardly bring myself to give a fuck, really.

Which is a shame, because there is so much to admire in the world he has constructed. The novel begins in Milwaukee in 1932, with the Depression in full swing, Prohibition nearing its end, and the criminal enterprises of the Midwest flourishing in the spaces between legitimate business and organized crime. Our protagonist, Hicks McTaggart, a former strikebreaker turned private detective, is sent after the runaway heiress of a Wisconsin cheese fortune and eventually finds himself carried across the Atlantic into a Europe increasingly troubled by fascism and political intrigue.

And I love how beautifully Pynchon resurrects that world. One gets the impression that he must have spent years rummaging through old newspapers, advertisements, political pamphlets, and forgotten municipal histories, collecting all those little particulars of an age which rarely survive in our collective imagination. The dance halls, the speakeasies, the German-American neighborhoods, the criminal enterprises, the enormous variety of people trying to make a living through means both legitimate and otherwise. There’s something almost archaeological about his writing, though what he excavates is hardly dead. His cities are wonderfully noisy, crowded with incidental lives which seem to continue well beyond the pages allotted to them. I felt at times that I’d been transported into the 1930s, not the decade as it appears in retrospect, already burdened with our knowledge of everything terrible to come, but the decade as it might have appeared to someone living through it, full of uncertainty, ridiculous fashions, and ordinary people going about their business while history quietly prepared to swallow them.

What interested me most was Pynchon’s treatment of the political atmosphere of the period, particularly the way he traces the emergence of fascism through institutions which had already become accustomed to exercising violence in the service of private interests. Hicks’s former occupation as a strikebreaker is important here. He spent years beating union workers on behalf of their employers, apparently without giving much thought to the politics of what he was doing, and later encounters an old associate from those days at a Nazi bowling alley. I thought this was one of the more effective moments in the novel, not least because it suggests that the distance between ordinary American labor repression and European fascism might have been considerably smaller than either Hicks or the reader would like to imagine. His old acquaintance has simply found another organization willing to give his violence a political purpose. Hicks, meanwhile, regards himself as reformed, though his reluctance to examine his own history makes that distinction rather difficult to accept.

I also liked the bowling alley itself, where the local Nazi sympathizers gather to drink beer, socialize, and congratulate one another on the glorious future awaiting them. The scene is funny, but there’s something unsettling about how comfortably their politics have been accommodated within the ordinary rituals of community life. These aren’t men standing apart from society, preparing some clandestine revolution in a cellar; they’re enjoying themselves in a recreational club, their convictions apparently no more disruptive to the occasion than a disagreement over the bowling scores. I think Pynchon is quite good at showing how political extremism can become familiar long before it becomes powerful, and how easily people mistake the vulgarity of its adherents for evidence that they cannot possibly be dangerous.

The novel eventually takes a much more dramatic departure from history, turning the Wisconsin milk strikes into the beginnings of a political crisis which culminates in a coup against Roosevelt and the installation of General MacArthur as the head of a military regime. It’s an interesting counterfactual, especially considering MacArthur’s actual involvement in suppressing the Bonus Army marchers in 1932, and Pynchon makes the alliance between financial interests, organized crime, and state violence seem less like an entirely invented conspiracy than an exaggeration of relationships which already existed. The historical materials are certainly there. What I found less convincing was the way the novel gradually makes its political argument so explicit that the earlier, more unsettling observations begin to lose some of their force.

I think the novel is at its most interesting when it suggests that the machinery of fascism need not be invented from nothing, that much of the necessary violence has already been organized, legitimized, and put to work under other names. Hicks didn’t need to become a Nazi to participate in the repression of working people; he only needed a paycheck. The businessmen and federal agents who regard Roosevelt as a threat to their interests don’t need to reject the existing political order so much as defend the parts of it which have always served them. There is something much more disturbing about these continuities than about the eventual spectacle of a military takeover, which makes the danger unmistakable in a way that ordinary political life seldom does. I found myself wishing Pynchon had remained a little longer with the former, with the gradual accommodations and small acts of complicity through which a society becomes receptive to authoritarian politics, rather than carrying everything toward a catastrophe which announces its own significance so plainly.

Perhaps this is also why the European sections were less compelling to me. The novel moves through the political instability of the former Austro-Hungarian territories, among nationalists, fascist paramilitaries, Soviet agents, and people trying to find some tolerable existence amid the collapse of the old order. There is an extraordinary amount of historical material here, though I often felt that the individual political movements became difficult to distinguish from the general commotion of the narrative. I don’t mean that their ideological differences are ignored, but that the pace of the novel gives the reader little opportunity to consider them before another conspiracy or confrontation intervenes. And because we already know where European history is headed, the accumulating signs of catastrophe can begin to feel rather predetermined. The future casts such an enormous shadow over these people that their present seems to exist chiefly as evidence of what we know is coming.

I understand that Hicks’s bewilderment is central to the novel, and that his longing for the familiar comforts of Milwaukee is deliberately ironic. He imagines returning to an America untouched by the political madness of Europe, apparently forgetting the strikebreaking violence, the Nazi sympathizers, the gangsters, and the federal agents who were already making his hometown considerably less innocent than he remembers it. But I think this is also where my frustration with the novel’s political commentary lies. The irony is established so early and repeated so insistently that I began to feel I was being shown the same historical lesson in a succession of increasingly elaborate disguises. I don’t find the comparison between the interwar years and our contemporary political climate uninteresting, but I did find it a little too easy to recognize what Pynchon wanted me to see.

Still, I can’t quite dislike Shadow Ticket. Pynchon’s historical curiosity remains astonishing, and I know few writers who can make the forgotten machinery of an era feel so wonderfully alive. There are passages where the streets of Milwaukee seem almost to breathe, and I found myself considerably more interested in the obscure histories behind its businesses, neighborhoods, and criminal enterprises than in the detective story ostensibly holding everything together. Perhaps that’s why the novel disappointed me as much as it did. Its historical imagination is so rich, and its political concerns so clearly informed by a lifetime of thinking about the relationship between power and violence, that I wanted to find its vision of the 1930s more troubling than I ultimately did.

I think I enjoyed inhabiting the world of Shadow Ticket considerably more than I enjoyed reading the novel itself. Pynchon made me feel wonderfully acquainted with the streets of a city I’ve never known, but I came away less haunted by the political nightmare he imagined than by the ordinary historical circumstances from which he’d constructed it. And perhaps that’s the greater horror anyway, that so much of what makes his fictional catastrophe possible required very little invention at all.
