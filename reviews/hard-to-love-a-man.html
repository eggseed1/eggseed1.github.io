Hard to Love a Man

ArtistThe Magnolia Electric Co
TypeEP
Released11 October 2005

Some of Molina’s greatest work is buried in this EP. Dare I call it the best EP ever made? Maybe. I’m too lazy to go through every track individually, but a few deserve special mention.

Hard to Love a Man

Originally appearing on What Comes After the Blues, “Hard to Love a Man” isn’t exclusive to this release, but I’m glad he reissued it as it’s one of my favorite songs he ever wrote

Folk can be an intensely self-indulgent form, so often circling the same private center -- my hurt, my leaving, my version of what the leaving meant --that what feels so fresh here is Molina’s effort to edge beyond that first-person enclosure, turning instead toward the inward weather of someone who has had to live beside him, after him, or around the damage he has made. The song’s central act is therefore not confession but displacement, the attempt to feel one’s own life from the other side of the wound and to imagine the full shape of harm one may have caused.

Knowing something of his history with difficult love, it is hard not to hear remorse moving through the song without ever announcing itself as such, though Molina seems less interested in the clean relief of confession than in the more humiliating exercise of imagining what it might have been like to love him… to remain close enough for tenderness to survive even as loving itself became a form of endurance. The song grants him no redemption for attempting this reversal; if anything, its force lies in how little the attempt appears to absolve him.

Who, exactly, is doing that enduring remains beautifully unclear. The voice is never securely fixed (is it a lover? a wife? a friend? a family member?) and because Molina withholds the relationship that might otherwise explain the feeling, the song slips free of autobiography into something harder to contain: the peculiar grief of caring for someone whose capacity to hurt you has become knotted up with the very reasons you cannot quite stop caring for them.

The arrangement seems to preserve that uncertainty rather than resolve it, the pedal steel slipping along the song’s edges with a thin, almost phosphorescent unease while the female vocal alters the center of gravity altogether; once the words pass into her voice, they no longer sound entirely his, but something heard back from the far side of whatever happened between them, changed by the distance they have crossed.

31 Seasons in the Minor League

An original found only on this EP, and still one of the most overlooked song in Molina’s catalog, “31 Seasons in the Minor League” continues on with the theme of defeat, the exhausted condition that follows once failure has become ordinary, when the promise of advancement has thinned to the point of disbelief but the rituals built around it continue anyway, asking for the same patience, the same labor, the same outward gestures of faith from people who no longer quite expect the promised future to arrive.

“You ain’t ever gonna win the game / Hell, kid, you don’t know the coach’s name.”

Molina so often works through a distinctly American vocabulary -- the railroad, the hard work, the team, the town you are meant to leave behind, some better place always waiting a little farther on -- and what gives those images their weight is how closely they belong to the old grammar of the American Dream, in which movement is supposed to mean progress, work to earn security, perseverance eventually to become arrival. In his songs, though, those objects tend to remain after the faith invested in them has begun to disappear, leaving the scenery of aspiration standing long after anyone can quite believe in where it was supposed to lead.

That is what makes the minor leagues such a cruelly exact image. Thirty-one seasons of showing up, waiting, improving, performing patience, and still the possibility of being called up never quite becomes a future so much as a ritual one continues to organize a life around. By then the question is no longer simply whether you are good enough to win, but whether winning was ever really available to you at all. Are ya losin’ because you played poorly, or because you were never really part of the game to begin with?

You can stop believing in the fairness of the game and still be made to experience its outcomes as personal failure, still measure yourself against standards whose legitimacy you no longer entirely accept. The system no longer needs your faith in order to keep assigning you a place within it; disbelief itself becomes something you carry privately while continuing to show up for another season.

One Thin Dime (Black Ram Session)

Molina’s version of the old “East St. Louis Blues,” known to be first recorded by Furry Lewis, follows Blind Willie McTell’s rendition particularly closely, inheriting much of its language; yet in Molina’s mouth those words sound almost uncannily his own.

“And I laid my head in a barroom door / And I can’t get drunk, drunk no more.”

It is difficult to hear a line like that in Molina’s voice without his own alcoholism entering the room, the lyric seeming to fit him with such painful ease that what was inherited begins to feel almost autobiographical.

That is partly what makes his relationship with old blues music so moving to me: I imagine those songs offered him a vocabulary for despair that had existed long before he did, words in which he could recognize something of himself without having first to invent the language for it. I deeply relate to that impulse -- the peculiar consolation of discovering that somebody decades before you had already arrived at the same exhaustion and somehow managed to put it into words; the feeling may be yours, but the language reminds you that it has never belonged to you alone.

Molina performed “East St. Louis Blues” in several forms, but this Black Ram session remains my favorite. He neither treats the song as an artifact to be carefully reproduced nor tries to disguise its age, instead drawing it into that lonely, spacious alt-country sound he understood so well. As a huge fan of both Blind Willie Mctell and Jason Molina, this cover is so endearing to me..
