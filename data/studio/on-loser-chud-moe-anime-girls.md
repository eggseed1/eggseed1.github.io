I’ve been reading a fair amount of sloppy, cute slice-of-life manga and light novels this week, mostly to relieve some of the general bleakness of being alive, which is rather funny considering how many of them concern girls whose lives are considerably more miserable than mine. One of these was WataMote, about a severely socially inept high school girl, Tomoko Kuroki, whose attempts at becoming popular invariably end in some humiliation so excruciating that I found myself having to put the book down every few pages. It’s ostensibly a comedy, though I can’t say I found it particularly funny. There’s a point at which secondhand embarrassment becomes almost physically unpleasant, and Tomoko reaches it with remarkable consistency. But who cares, she’s a cute moe anime girl!!!

Tomoko is vain, resentful, occasionally quite unpleasant, and so incapable of ordinary social interaction that even the simplest conversation becomes a personal catastrophe. Yet all of this is rendered through the visual language of cuteness, her failures made endearing by the very qualities which would make her rather difficult to tolerate in real life. I kept wondering how differently the same character would be received if she weren’t a cute anime girl, if her social incompetence were simply incompetence, without the aesthetic consolation of being adorable. There is something wonderfully convenient about the arrangement, the way a person’s deficiencies can become objects of affection once they’ve been sufficiently removed from the practical difficulties of actually having to live with them.

Cuteness has a rather unpleasant little underside which Sianne Ngai explores in Our Aesthetic Categories, where the tenderness we feel toward small, helpless things is bound up with a certain desire to handle, diminish, and exercise power over them. A stuffed animal invites affection partly because it cannot resist being squeezed, and the weakness which makes it adorable also makes it available for whatever indignities we wish to inflict upon it. The same logic becomes considerably less innocent when applied to fictional women, whose inability to function can become not merely a defect we’re willing to forgive, but the very reason we desire them. The girl remains helpless, the spectator affectionately superior, and both are apparently satisfied with this arrangement. One can enjoy her vulnerability without ever being inconvenienced by it.

FX Senshi Kurumi-chan takes this to a rather entertaining extreme, following a girl who attempts to recover the money her late mother lost through foreign-exchange trading, only to become consumed by the same destructive habits. The losses are enormous, the consequences increasingly miserable, and yet the girls remain irresistibly cute throughout the entire ordeal. Financial ruin is presented through the same cheerful visual language one might associate with girls eating cake or going shopping, and the prospect of another catastrophic trading decision becomes practically an attraction in itself. I suppose the joke would hardly work if Kurumi were a sensible person making sensible financial decisions, but there’s something almost ingenious about making her incompetence the principal source of entertainment. The viewer is free to find her financial irresponsibility charming without ever having to lend her money.

There’s a whole category of female characters whose shortcomings seem to constitute their appeal. The stupid girl, the clumsy girl, the girl who can’t talk to people, the girl whose life is an unending succession of humiliating little disasters. Male protagonists can of course be equally pathetic, though their inadequacies are more often employed as the initial condition from which some eventual improvement is expected. The boy is weak because he must become strong; his failures establish the distance between who he is and who he might become. For certain female characters, however, incompetence is something to be preserved, since her improvement might threaten the very qualities for which she was liked in the first place. I suppose this isn’t so far removed from the older baka character or the dumb blonde of Western comedies, except that mere stupidity no longer seems sufficient. Now she must be socially incapacitated, financially irresponsible, perhaps so thoroughly maladjusted that even the most unsuccessful viewer can imagine himself as the comparatively competent party. There is something rather depressing about a fantasy in which the woman must become progressively less capable for the person desiring her to feel more necessary.

And the affection involved isn’t necessarily as innocent as it appears. Ngai’s account of cuteness leaves open the uncomfortable possibility that tenderness and aggression might not be opposites at all, that the pleasure of protecting something helpless can exist alongside the pleasure of exercising power over it. The distinction becomes interesting when one considers ryona, a category of Japanese fetish imagery centered on the suffering and physical defeat of fictional characters, frequently women. Here the vulnerability which moe ordinarily dresses in tenderness becomes a more direct object of fascination. The character’s suffering is no longer simply an unfortunate condition which makes her deserving of affection, but part of what the spectator has come to see. I wouldn’t suggest that enjoying Tomoko’s humiliations is equivalent to enjoying fictional violence, much less actual violence, but the distance between pitying a character’s helplessness and finding that helplessness entertaining is not always as great as we’d like to imagine. Even the ordinary pleasure of watching a cute girl fail contains some small element of cruelty, since the very misfortune which makes us feel sorry for her is also what keeps us reading. We sympathize with her predicament while secretly hoping she’ll find herself in another one.

I had a similar discomfort watching A Silent Voice, although it’s a considerably more serious work and I don’t mean to suggest that it treats disability in quite the same way. The film follows Shoya, a boy who bullied a deaf girl named Shoko in elementary school and later attempts to reconcile with her. My frustration was less with the depiction of her deafness itself than with how closely her suffering becomes bound to the boy’s redemption. His guilt, isolation, and eventual capacity for tenderness are given an elaborate emotional progression, while Shoko’s gentleness and tendency to blame herself make her particularly suited to the work of forgiving him. She has her own anguish and desires, certainly, and the film does give them attention, but I found myself resenting how much of its emotional structure depended on what her suffering made possible for him. There is something uncomfortable about a woman being granted enormous emotional significance while remaining, in so many respects, the occasion for someone else’s transformation.

Tomoko’s social incompetence, Kurumi’s addiction, and Shoko’s disability are obviously not interchangeable, nor do I think every depiction of female vulnerability must be understood as an invitation to enjoy her powerlessness. Fiction would be terribly dull if its characters were required to be perfectly competent, and I don’t particularly want to read about women who never make fools of themselves. But there is a distinction between finding someone lovable despite her deficiencies and finding those deficiencies necessary to her lovability. I think the latter carries a much more troubling implication, especially when the affection depends on her remaining conveniently incapable of improving her circumstances.

Of course, I’ve been writing as though the spectator were necessarily male, which is a little presumptuous considering that I enjoy these characters myself. I don’t think every person who finds Tomoko cute is secretly fantasizing about dominating helpless women. Sometimes it’s simply comforting to see someone whose failures resemble your own being treated with affection, and I certainly recognize that pleasure. But I find it difficult to separate this comfort from the knowledge that the qualities being celebrated are often the very ones which make living difficult. I enjoy watching these girls fail partly because their failures are familiar to me, and because fiction offers the rather pleasant possibility that such failures might make a person more lovable rather than less.

Margaret Atwood writes in The Robber Bride: “You are a woman with a man inside watching a woman. You are your own voyeur.” I’ve always found that passage rather dreadful, not least because I recognize something of myself in it. There is a kind of self-consciousness that comes from being so accustomed to anticipating another person’s judgment that eventually one begins to experience oneself through it. Even when nobody is watching, I occasionally find myself presenting my own inadequacies in ways I hope might be regarded as endearing, as though being unable to do something becomes less humiliating if I can make it look cute. And the embarrassing thing is that I don’t always know whether I genuinely find these qualities appealing or whether I’ve simply learned to prefer the version of myself which I imagine someone else might want.

Judith Butler, in The Psychic Life of Power, describes how power participates in the formation of the very subject it governs, shaping not only what a person is permitted to do but the desires through which she seeks recognition. There is something rather frightening about the possibility that one’s attachment to a particular form of subordination might be perfectly sincere, that the desire to be regarded as helpless or agreeable needn’t be imposed upon us by some external authority because we’ve already incorporated it into our understanding of what makes us desirable. It’s easy enough to resent the expectation that women should be conveniently incompetent, but considerably more difficult to recognize how much satisfaction one might have learned to derive from being perceived that way. There needn’t be anyone watching me for me to continue arranging myself for an audience.

I dislike the romanticization of female helplessness, and yet I find a certain solace in seeing girls whose shortcomings resemble my own being treated as lovable. Sometimes I even catch myself hoping that my negative qualities might be attractive to someone, that my awkwardness or inability to manage certain aspects of my life could be regarded with the same tenderness afforded to these fictional girls. I can recognize the condescension involved and still want to be its beneficiary. The desire to be loved is not always terribly concerned with the conditions under which that love is offered.

There’s something of Lauren Berlant’s cruel optimism in this, the attachment to a fantasy of happiness which quietly obstructs the possibility of attaining it. If my failures are charming, then overcoming them begins to seem almost unnecessary. I need only find someone who enjoys them, and the unpleasant business of improving myself can be postponed indefinitely. I’ve confused being loved despite my faults with being loved because of them, and the distinction is rather important. The former would allow me to change without fearing that I’d become less desirable, while the latter gives me a reason to remain exactly as I am. It is a terribly convenient fantasy, provided one doesn’t mind spending the rest of one’s life being the sort of person who needs to be forgiven for existing.

I don’t particularly want to be helpless. I just want to believe that the things I dislike about myself don’t make me unlovable, which is a perfectly ordinary desire, though I’ve somehow managed to turn it into a fantasy in which my shortcomings become the very basis of another person’s affection. There’s something a little humiliating about wanting permission to remain miserable, provided someone else finds the misery charming.

Anyway, I still like Tomoko. I think she’s cute.
